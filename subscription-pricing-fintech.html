Mikaal Abdulla on Subscription Pricing in Fintech: Why the Model Works and Where It Falls Short

📅 October 6, 2026
✎ Mikaal Abdulla
⌚ 3 min read

Mikaal Abdulla brought subscription pricing to retail investing in Asia when 8 Securities introduced a flat, recurring fee in place of per-trade commissions, a Netflix-style model applied to financial services. It was a regional first, and it raised a question still debated across fintech: when does a subscription serve the customer better than paying per transaction?

Abdulla, a fintech executive and startup founder whose career spans Asia Pacific, Europe, and the Middle East, approached the question from the customer's side. Commission pricing taxes activity. For a new investor building a position gradually, every small trade carries a cost that can discourage the habits a platform should encourage. The pricing question, in other words, was also a question about which investor behaviors a platform rewards.

Why a Flat Fee Changes Investor Behavior

A subscription separates the cost of access from the cost of activity. Once the monthly fee is paid, an investor can rebalance, add to a position, or make small regular purchases without calculating whether each trade is worth its commission. The model aligns the platform's interest with consistent engagement rather than with the number of trades it can generate, a meaningful shift in incentives for a brokerage.

The model pairs naturally with other mobile-first features. Fractional shares let investors buy small amounts of expensive stocks, and a robo-advisor rebalances automatically. Under per-trade pricing, both generate frequent small transactions that commissions penalize. Under a subscription, they become part of the product rather than a cost to manage.

Where the Model Falls Short

Subscription pricing is not universally better. For an investor who trades a few times a year, a recurring fee can cost more than occasional commissions. A provider must also deliver ongoing value, because a subscription that feels idle is the first expense a customer cancels. Clear communication about who the model suits, and who it does not, is part of applying it responsibly.

There is a business-model risk as well. Recurring revenue depends on retention, which puts pressure on product quality, content, and service every month. Pricing becomes a continuous commitment rather than a single decision. For a startup, that pressure can be healthy. It forces continuous attention to whether customers are getting what they pay for, rather than relying on transaction volume to mask weak engagement.

Pricing as a Product Decision

Subscription pricing at 8 Securities was a product decision as much as a commercial one. It followed the same reasoning as the platform's mobile-only design and its robo-advisory and social trading features, all part of the mobile-first brokerage model. Each was built around how retail investors in high-growth markets actually behave.

That perspective draws on Mikaal Abdulla's decade at E*TRADE, where pricing had to work across 14 markets with different cost structures, and on his background as a fintech founder. He explores related product strategy topics in his long-form writing on WordPress.

Conclusion

Subscription pricing works best when it removes friction from behaviors that benefit the investor: regular contributions, diversification, and long-term holding. It falls short when the recurring fee outpaces the value delivered. For founders designing consumer financial products, the lesson from Asia is to treat pricing as part of the product itself.
